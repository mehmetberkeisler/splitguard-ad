% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\abstract{Patient-wise splitting is the standard remedy for leakage in medical
imaging, and its guarantee is usually credited to the splitting procedure.
It is not a property of the procedure. It is a property of the participant
identifier the procedure is given, and identifiers are deleted, fragmented
and merged by redistribution. We measure that dependence across its full
range on ADNI1 ($220$ cognitively normal and AD participants, $1{,}123$
scans, one 2D coronal-centre slice per scan). With identifiers complete,
subject-wise splitting reports an area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUROC) of $0.771$; with every identifier removed the
same unchanged procedure reports $0.946$, against $0.947$ for random
image-level splitting, an inflation of $+0.176$ accrued without any error
being raised. A provenance-aware grouping adds nothing while identifiers
are intact, which is the design target, and declines optimism in $7$ of
$8$ degraded conditions (Wilcoxon $p = 0.008$). Permuting diagnosis across
participants removes the disease signal, yet random splitting still
reaches $0.878$ AUROC while both patient-aware protocols sit at chance,
identifying patient recall as the mechanism. One instance is observed
rather than imposed: a widely used public 2D AD benchmark is, by content
matching, a redistribution of $200$ OASIS-1 participants, and grouping by
the only identifier it supplies still leaves $70\%$ of test images sharing
a participant with training. Manifests, audit code and the rebuild
pipeline are released under Apache-2.0.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Deep-learning models for Alzheimer's disease (AD) classification from
structural MRI routinely report near-perfect benchmark performance that does
not survive patient-independent evaluation~\cite{wen2020,yagis2021,diagnostics2025}.
The accepted remedy is to split by patient, and the guarantee that follows is
usually credited to the splitting procedure.

Patient identity is one shortcut among several that a model can take instead of
learning pathology~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut,degrave2021shortcuts,zech2018variable},
and it is the one a split is supposed to close. Leakage of this kind is a
recognised contributor to the reproducibility problem in applied machine
learning~\cite{kapoor2023leakage,varoquaux2022failures,oakden2020hidden,roberts2021pitfalls}.

It is not a property of the procedure. A procedure can only keep a patient's
images together if it is told which images belong to that patient, and
participant identifiers are deleted, fragmented and merged whenever a cohort is
redistributed. Patient-wise splitting is therefore a property of the identifier
the procedure is given, and the strength of the guarantee varies with the
quality of that evidence.

The two ends of this axis are established. Random image-level splitting is
known to inflate, on individual AD cohorts~\cite{wen2020,yagis2021}, in
longitudinal brain MRI~\cite{rumala2023}, and in connectome
models~\cite{rosenblatt2024}; patient-wise splitting is known to correct it, and
tools exist to enforce it~\cite{datasail2025}. What lies between has not been
measured. That is the interval redistributed benchmarks occupy, and
\S\ref{sec:observed} shows one of them sitting in it.

Two concurrent audits reach that interval from the other direction, by finding
benchmarks already there. Sifat \etal~\cite{aljuhani2026leakageaware} trace
subject identifiers from the slice captions of a Kaggle redistribution of
OASIS-1 and report that its subject count does not match the labelled source
cohort. Vangala \etal~\cite{scoresthathold2026} recover patient identity for the
traceable $12.6\%$ of a public brain-tumour test split, find that $95.5\%$ of
that subset shares a participant with training, and report that removing every
near-duplicate test image leaves balanced accuracy unchanged. We take both as
evidence that the condition is common rather than exotic. Neither measures the
path between the endpoints, and neither retrains.

\paragraph{Contributions}
First, and principally, we measure the function relating evaluation validity to
identifier quality on one cohort in one code state. With identifiers complete,
subject-wise splitting reports $0.771$ area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUROC); with every identifier removed the same unchanged
procedure reports $0.946$, against $0.947$ for the random image-level splitting
it exists to prevent, an inflation of $+0.176$ accrued without the procedure
raising an error at any point. Second, we separate patient recall from disease
signal with a permutation control, an established
instrument~\cite{chaibubneto2017permutation}, and locate the regime in which a
provenance-aware grouping helps. Third, we release \SGA{}, an executable audit
that builds a per-image leakage graph from whatever identifier evidence a cohort
carries. We offer it as the instrument that makes the measurement possible
rather than as an improvement on participant grouping: where identity is intact
its component layer reduces to participant grouping, as it should, and the
benefit it carries appears only once provenance degrades. Both halves of that
statement are reported as results.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Cohorts}
\label{sec:cohorts}

\textbf{ADNI1} (Tier 3) is the headline cohort, used under an active LONI data
use agreement~\cite{adni,jack2008adnimri,petersen2010adniclinical}. The
collection holds $2{,}182$ pre-processed 1.5T T1-weighted volumes from $382$
participants; the CN-versus-AD primary arm is $220$ participants and $1{,}123$
diagnosis-resolved scans, with converter components excluded. Each volume is
reduced to one 2D coronal-centre slice at ingest. Labels join imaging to the
clinical table on \texttt{(RID, VISCODE)}, with a 180-day date-proximity
fallback for $22.2\%$ of scans; Supplementary Material~S5 accounts for that join
per scan.

\textbf{OASIS-1} (Tier 2) is the replication cohort, where participant
identifiers are authoritative~\cite{oasis1}. It is operationally external to
ADNI: different site, scanners, protocols and recruitment era. The
direction-preservation we report between the two is a partial cross-site check,
not external validation in the regulatory sense.

\textbf{A redistributed public benchmark} (Tier 1) is the observed instance.
It is a widely used 2D AD benchmark of $6{,}400$ images in four CDR-derived
classes. By content matching against OASIS-1 we recover its complete
participant mapping: all $6{,}400$ images resolve unambiguously to $200$
OASIS-1 participants at $32$ consecutive axial slices each, with the weakest
correct match correlating at $0.9996$ against a strongest cross-participant
match of $0.9623$. Supplementary Material~S8 gives the mapping audit. Because
all $200$ participants are also in our Tier-2 set, the cross-tier replication
rests on two source cohorts rather than three.

\subsection{The leakage graph and the three protocols}
\label{sec:leakage_graph}

\SGA{} builds an undirected graph over images and joins two images whenever any
surviving identifier says they belong to one participant: the same subject key,
session, series UID, or acquisition date scoped within a participant. Missing
values are treated as absent evidence rather than as a shared key, which matters
because a literal \texttt{unknown} used as an identifier would merge every
unlabelled scan into one fictional participant and the audit would then pass the
split. Connected components of this graph are the indivisible units, and a
class-stratified bin-packer allocates whole components to partitions.

The same reasoning applies to the grouped splitters in general-purpose
libraries and medical-imaging frameworks~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn,cardoso2022monai}:
each takes a group label as input and is only as reliable as that label.

Three protocols are compared on the same image universe. \textbf{Protocol A}
shuffles images at random and is the leaky protocol. \textbf{Protocol B} splits
by the supplied subject identity with no component constraint. \textbf{Protocol
C} is the component-safe split. All three use the same assigner, so a
difference between them is attributable to the grouping and not to the
assignment step. Where identity is complete, B and C coincide by construction.

\subsection{Degrading and corrupting provenance}
\label{sec:degradation_design}

Provenance integrity is varied in two ways on the ADNI primary arm, at five
seeds each. Identifiers are \emph{deleted} per scan at rates
$\{0, 10, 25, 50, 100\}\%$, which models metadata loss. They are separately
\emph{corrupted} in the two ways redistribution corrupts them: \textsc{split}
deals one participant across several keys, and \textsc{merge} places several
participants under one, each at intensity
$\lambda \in \{0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1\}$. True identities are retained out of
band, so leakage is scored against ground truth rather than against the damaged
key. These are controlled stress tests of identifiable failure modes, not
estimates of their prevalence in clinical repositories.

Training is held fixed throughout: ResNet-18 with ImageNet initialisation,
$15$ epochs, AdamW at $10^{-4}$, class-balanced loss, seeds
$\{0,1,2,3,4\}$, deterministic kernels and seeded loaders. Supplementary
Material~S1 gives the full configuration. Intervals are bootstrap
intervals~\cite{efron1986bootstrap,carpenter2000bootstrap} over five seeds and
are descriptive throughout, not significance tests. Every AUROC reported here comes from
one code state, approximately \GpuHours{}~GPU-hours in total; the package list
frozen on the final node is released as \texttt{runs\_gpu/\pb gpu\_environment.txt},
and \texttt{scripts/\pb verify\_paper\_numbers.py} holds every number in this
manuscript to the artefact that produced it.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Reference: the gap under intact provenance}
\label{sec:reference}

On the ADNI1 primary arm, random image-level splitting reports
\AdniRandomAUROC{} AUROC and component-safe splitting \AdniSafeAUROC{}, an
inflation gap of \AdniGapTotal{} (paired-seed interval \AdniGapCI{},
hierarchical \AdniHierCI{}; $n=5$, descriptive). The gap survives every
pre-specified variation of cohort, backbone, converter rule, acquisition type,
input dimensionality and label-linkage strictness
(Figure~\ref{fig:arm_forest}): \AdniDenseGap{} under DenseNet-121,
\OasisGapTotal{} on OASIS-1, \VolGapTotal{} on a 3D volumetric arm, and
\AdniGapExactKey{} when the weaker quarter of the label chain is dropped. No
arm's interval includes zero.

Decomposed, the whole of that effect is the subject layer. The component
marginal, Protocol~B minus Protocol~C, includes zero in every arm. That is the
expected result and the control for everything that follows: with authoritative
identifiers a provenance-aware grouping should agree with participant grouping,
and it does.

\subsection{The mechanism is patient recall}
\label{sec:permutation}

The gap alone cannot separate ``the leaky protocol learned the disease better''
from ``the leaky protocol recognised its patients''. We separate them by
removing the disease. The instrument is not new: Chaibub Neto
\etal~\cite{chaibubneto2017permutation} introduced permutation tests for exactly
this purpose, and label-shuffling controls of the same shape are now standard in
neurophysiology benchmarks.

Diagnosis labels are permuted across participants, once over the whole cohort so
that a participant straddling a boundary carries the same permuted label on both
sides, and the three protocols are retrained unchanged. Random splitting still
reaches $0.878$ AUROC. Subject-only sits at $0.521$ and component-safe at
$0.427$, both chance. The permutation is verified to have taken effect: permuted
labels coincide with the true diagnosis $48.3\%$, $55.1\%$ and $50.3\%$ of the
time, which is the chance rate. All three protocols fit the training set equally
well; only the random split's memorisation transfers, because only its test set
contains the same people. A model with no pathology available to learn cannot be
exploiting pathology. That structural MRI carries recoverable identity is
independently established~\cite{deepbrainprint2023,wachinger2015brainprint,schwarz2019face};
what the control adds is that a classifier trained for diagnosis uses it.

\subsection{Evaluation validity as a function of identifier quality}
\label{sec:provenance_degradation}

Deleting identifiers at controlled rates traces the function
(Figure~\ref{fig:provenance_degradation}, Table~\ref{tab:provenance_degradation}).
At zero loss the two patient-aware protocols admit no leakage and are
indistinguishable, reproducing \S\ref{sec:reference}. They separate immediately
thereafter: at $10\%$ loss the graph prevents $49\%$ of the participant leakage
subject-wise splitting admits ($9.4$ against $18.6$ straddling participants),
and $30$ to $37\%$ at higher rates. The amplification is non-linear. Deleting
$10\%$ of identifiers returns $36\%$ of test participants to training under
Protocol~B, because a participant contributes a median of five scans and one of
them losing its index is enough to detach that scan from the group.

Following the AUROC to the end of the sweep gives the result this paper is built
on. With identifiers complete, subject-wise splitting reports $0.771$
$[0.709, 0.833]$. With every identifier removed, the same procedure, unchanged,
reports $0.946$ $[0.921, 0.972]$. The random image-level split of
\S\ref{sec:reference} reports $0.947$ $[0.926, 0.965]$ on the same cohort. The
difference between the last two is $0.001$ and their intervals overlap across
almost their whole length.

We state that as a failure to distinguish rather than as an equality: five seeds
cannot establish that two procedures agree. What the comparison does establish
is that nothing in the reported number separates them. A reader handed the
right-hand column cannot tell that the split was meant to be patient-disjoint,
and neither can the procedure, which raises no error: every scan is a singleton,
every singleton is its own group, and grouping by group is vacuous. The
inflation across the sweep is $+0.176$ AUROC, accrued without a warning at any
point.

That is the sense in which patient-wise splitting is not a property of a
splitting algorithm. The algorithm is unchanged along this axis. Only the
evidence it is given changes, and the guarantee it is credited with degrades
continuously into the condition it was adopted to prevent.

\subsection{Structured corruption: where the graph earns its place}
\label{sec:structured_corruption}

Deletion is the visible failure. Redistribution corrupts identifiers invisibly,
and \textsc{split} and \textsc{merge} model the two directions
(Table~\ref{tab:stress_structural}). Under fragmentation the graph recovers most
of what the participant key lost, because session and series edges do not take
the subject key as an input: at $\lambda = 1$ it prevents $90\%$ of the
straddling participants subject-wise splitting admits ($6.4$ against $61.6$).
Under merging it prevents nothing, and nothing is the correct answer:
over-grouping destroys the participant count without placing any participant on
both sides of a boundary, so there is no leakage to prevent.

Across the eight degraded cells where a difference is defined, the component
grouping declined optimism the subject-only grouping accepted in seven, with a
mean of $+0.026$ AUROC and a one-sided Wilcoxon $p = 0.008$. The cells share
seeds and a base manifest, so this is a consistent direction rather than an
independent test, and we report it as such. Taken with \S\ref{sec:reference},
where the same layer buys nothing on intact data, the pattern is the one the
framework predicts: the graph is worth its cost exactly where some identifier
other than the participant key survives.

\subsection{An instance observed rather than imposed}
\label{sec:observed}

The deletion sweep is synthetic. Tier~1 is the same condition found in the wild
(Table~\ref{tab:tier1_rules}). Its filename index is not a participant
identifier: $324$ distinct keys divide into $149$ that match exactly one
participant and $175$ that merge two or three, while $179$ of the $200$
participants are spread across two or more keys and one across $32$. Grouping by
that key, the only identifier the release supplies, leaves
\TierOneShareFilename{} of test images sharing a participant with training.

What that costs depends on which protocol a user adopts. Random splitting, which
is what the published literature on this benchmark does, reports
\TierOneRandomAUROC{}. Grouping by the filename key reports
\TierOneFilenameAUROC{}, and grouping by the recovered participant identity
reports \TierOneTrueAUROC{}. The difference between the first and the last is
\TierOneGapTotal{} \TierOneGapCI{}. The broken key therefore recovers most of
the correction while leaving seven test images in ten contaminated, and the
reported accuracies on this benchmark are an artefact of random splitting rather
than of the damaged identifier. Both failures are invisible from inside the
release: recovering the mapping required matching the images against the source
cohort, which a user of the release is not placed to do.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Three provenance regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}

The results describe one structure. Patient-wise evaluation is not a property of
a splitting algorithm but of the identity evidence the algorithm is given, and
that evidence admits three regimes.

In \textbf{Regime I} provenance is intact and the graph is equivalent to
participant grouping. The null of \S\ref{sec:reference} is the design target: a
provenance-aware splitter that disagreed with participant grouping on clean data
would be wrong. In \textbf{Regime II} the participant key is damaged but other
relations survive, and the graph recovers part of what was lost: $49\%$ at
$10\%$ identifier loss, $90\%$ under full fragmentation. This is the paper's
subject. In \textbf{Regime III} no relation survives. Tier~1 is there, and so is
the right-hand end of the deletion sweep. The limit is informational, not a
software limitation, and we claim nothing for the graph in it.

The useful question is therefore not whether a provenance-aware splitter beats
participant grouping, but which regime a cohort is in, which can be answered
from identifiers alone before any model is trained.

\subsection{Why contamination audits disagree about inflation}
\label{sec:audits_disagree}

Vangala \etal~\cite{scoresthathold2026} report that removing every near-duplicate
test image leaves balanced accuracy unchanged, and read that as contamination
not surfacing as an inflated score. We read their result as compatible with
ours, and the difference is in what the two designs can detect.

Their comparison removes duplicated images from the test partition and re-scores
a model trained once; no retraining is involved, so the training split still
contains those images. A model that has memorised a participant retains that
memory after the participant's duplicated images are deleted from the test set.
And the identity channel is not confined to near-duplicates: in their own
corpus, $28.8\%$ of test images have a near-twin in training while $95.5\%$ of
the traceable ones share a \emph{participant} with training. Deleting the first
layer leaves the second. Their design measures whether duplicated test items are
easier to classify, which is a real question, and not whether the model learned
to recognise participants. Separating those requires an intervention on the
training side, which is what the protocol comparison and the permutation control
provide. Vangala \etal{} offer three candidate explanations for their null and
establish none; the permutation control discriminates between exactly those
alternatives.

The practical consequence is narrow and useful. A deduplication audit, the
field's common instrument, cannot detect participant-identity leakage, and a
benchmark that passes one has not been shown to be sound. Reporting checklists
ask whether data were split by patient~\cite{mongan2020claim,tripod2024} and
risk-of-bias instruments ask whether the split was appropriate~\cite{wolff2019probast};
neither asks what identifier the split was performed on, which the measurement
here suggests is the question that determines the answer.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The degradation is synthetic, imposed on a cohort with complete provenance
rather than observed on one that lost it, which is what makes ground-truth
scoring possible and also what makes it a model of the failure rather than the
failure itself. A cohort whose damage leaves partial provenance for the graph to
carry, Regime~II observed rather than induced, is the most informative next
experiment. Tier~1 is not that case: its redistribution removed the session and
series identifiers along with the participant one.

Replication on an external cohort such as
AIBL~\cite{ellis2009aibl}, UK Biobank~\cite{sudlow2015ukbiobank} or
OASIS-3~\cite{lamontagne2019oasis3} is the immediate next step and is not
claimed here. The cohort definitions follow the NIA-AA
framework~\cite{jack2011,jack2018niaaa}, and the architectures are
representative of the AD classification literature rather than
state-of-the-art~\cite{korolev2017residual,lian2020hierarchical,qiu2020explainable,liem2017brainage};
nothing in the measurement depends on the backbone, as the DenseNet-121 and
volumetric arms show. The public-health framing of early
detection~\cite{who2023} is context, not a claim this paper tests.

Every finding is a property of a 2D coronal-centre-slice representation, with a
3D volumetric arm indicating the shortcut survives whole-volume input and is in
fact larger there. All intervals are descriptive at five seeds, not significance
tests. The contamination dose-response reported in Supplementary Material~S4
describes a substitution procedure that also thins the training set and shifts
the evaluated class mix, so its slope of $+\DoseSlope{}$ is not a causal price
per unit of leakage. Checkpoint selection, subgroup disparity and the clinical
operating point are reported in Supplementary Material~S2, S6 and S10
respectively.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Patient-wise splitting is not a property of a splitting algorithm. It is a
property of the participant identifier the algorithm is given. With identifiers
complete the procedure reports $0.771$ AUROC on our primary arm; with every
identifier removed the same unchanged procedure reports $0.946$, against
$0.947$ for the random image-level splitting it exists to prevent, and no step
raises an error anywhere along that path. A study reporting that it split by
patient has reported a procedure, not a guarantee.

The endpoints of that axis are not in dispute. What has not been measured is the
interval between them, and that interval is where redistributed benchmarks sit:
one widely used public benchmark reports \TierOneRandomAUROC{} under the
splitting its literature uses and \TierOneTrueAUROC{} once participant identity
is recovered. Before trusting a patient-disjoint number, a reader should ask
what identifier produced it, and an audit that cannot answer that question has
not established soundness.
